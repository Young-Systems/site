> **Disclaimer: Follow your organization's internal security exception, escalation, and change management processes when handling requests like these.**

## The request
Have you ever received a request from a client of yours that seems to betray every one of your senses?

These requests might sound like:

- "Please disable MFA for our CEO, Bob Belcher"
- "Can you make passwords not required?"

You may receive another request that makes you wonder whether anyone retained the information from their security awareness training.

I can assure you that this happens to at least one IT professional in the world every ten minutes. Every three minutes if they are on their lunch break.

## Look for a safer alternative
While it may be tempting to respond with a reply so funny that HR would like to hear it themselves, the important thing is to understand the request and identify a solution that keeps the client aligned with applicable security policies, contractual requirements, regulatory obligations, and cyber insurance requirements.

Before treating the request as an unavoidable security exception, discuss safer alternatives and compensating controls. Depending on the situation, these might include:

1. Clarify the business problem behind the request.
2. Troubleshoot the underlying technical issue.
3. Propose safer alternatives and compensating controls.
4. Document the risk if the client rejects those alternatives.
5. Escalate the decision to an authorized risk owner.
6. Implement only the approved, limited exception.
7. Set an expiration date and review the exception later.

The goal is not necessarily to reject the client's request. It is to understand the business need and find the safest reasonable way to meet it.

A lot of the time, it's a relatively simple issue that can be fixed as easily. If it's truly an issue that cannot be fixed by clicking a button, you should escalate to your management and follow your change management process. Ideally, you don't want to remove any security layers that protect high-value targets, such as executives and privileged users.

## If the client still pushes the request
If it is a situation where after you've attempted to resolve the issue and the client is still pushing for this change, the next best step is to introduce compensating controls. Before approving a dangerous exception, discuss alternatives such as:

- Adjusting sign-in frequency
- Registering a managed device
- Using phishing-resistant authentication
- Restricting access by device, location, or application
- Increasing monitoring and alerting
- Limiting the exception's scope and duration

If the client rejects the safer alternatives, the request may need to enter your organization's formal risk acceptance process. This commonly involves documenting the request, your company's recommendations, the identified risks, any compensating controls, the authorized risk owner, and the exception's expiration date.

The client or authorized decision-maker can then formally acknowledge the risks and approve proceeding against your company's recommendation. Any contractual or liability language should be reviewed by authorized leadership or legal counsel.

## Final thoughts
The best thing we can do as IT professionals is provide clear recommendations based on security best practices and the client's business needs. If an authorized decision-maker chooses to accept the remaining risk, our responsibility is to document the decision, implement only the approved exception, apply reasonable compensating controls, and follow the appropriate internal processes.

We may not always control the final decision, but we can make sure it is an informed one.
