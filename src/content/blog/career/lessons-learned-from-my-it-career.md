## Some context
I've been working in IT for nearly five years. You may be asking, “Why should I listen to someone who is still so green in this career field?”

I am not here to say whether you should or you shouldn't. That is totally up to you! I just hope the lessons that I share in this document help you as you grow in your career in IT.

## Lesson 1. Evaluate IT Education Carefully
Information technology is one of those career fields that **practical experience often carries more weight than classroom experience**. Technology changes quickly, so a degree or certification should be treated as the beginning of your education - not the end of it.

While the schools provide a lot of fundamental knowledge, and may check *some* HR boxes, you will more than likely end up in unnecessary debt. *Ask me how I know.*

If I could go back and talk to a younger Sam, I would tell him not to enroll in that certification program. Instead, I would encourage him to build a home lab and save the $20,000 spent on a course that resulted in *maybe* three or four useful certifications.
> **Note:** You can build a home lab just by using any old computer. Even one that you buy off of Facebook Marketplace for $30 or from a family member that hasn't been updated since the early 2000s.  
>

## Lesson 2. Know your audience
Yes, this may sound like common sense, but it wasn't something I understood until later in my career.

At my first IT job, I had a whiff of cybersecurity, and that was all my mind was set on. Unfortunately, I pushed this too far with the IT leadership and caused some frustration and burned bridges by not understanding when to quit, or by talking to the *wrong people*.

At that point, I was on the short list when it came to company layoffs that occurred not too long afterward. 

You'll understand as you gain more influence and responsibility that keeping your mouth shut in certain instances can prevent larger issues from coming down the pipeline. 

Another example is communicating during a **security incident**. When speaking with users or clients, avoid declaring an account "compromised" or an incident a "breach" before the facts support that conclusion. Use precise language, explain what is known, and avoid creating unnecessary panic.

## Lesson 3. Ask questions (yes, even the "stupid questions")
You should ALWAYS ask questions. Some may disagree when I say "there are no stupid questions," but it's the truth. Asking questions is the best way to learn. As an escalation point, I would much rather hear the "stupid question" and ensure you understand the topic or the solution than not hearing any questions and being stuck with cleaning up the mess. 

Trust me: You'll save everyone time by asking questions.

On the topic of asking questions: You should ask to help with any project that "tickles your fancy."

A great example of this comes from my time on the help desk, where I assisted with tweaks to the **End User Onboarding/Offboarding** automation. Through that experience, I gained a better understanding of how scripting and automation tools such as **PowerShell** function in a production environment.
> Without this introduction into PowerShell, I don't think I'd be as proficient as I am today or able to build tools on the fly to assist with integration activities.

## Lesson 4. Protect Yourself with a Paper Trail
This is a big one.

To put it bluntly, documented communication is invaluable when a client chooses to proceed against your recommendation. If the decision causes a problem later, you can demonstrate what was recommended, what risks were communicated, and who approved the final course of action.
> Yes, this has happened before.

Obviously, you should **NOT** wish for anything to go wrong. However, as IT is a field where we collaborate with humans all the time, we always make mistakes. Sometimes those mistakes cause a security incident where an executive's account was accessed by a threat actor. Other times, they just cause technical debt that you'd need to resolve as soon as possible.

When possible, document the request and retain the revelant information, whether it's in a Teams chat, email, or a call recording:
- Approval
- Confirmation
- Direction
- Risk acknowledgment

Store that information in the appropriate ticketing system or technical documentation platform.

## Lesson 5. Document as much as you can
When you are introduced into a new environment, there may not always be documentation. So the only knowledge is tribal knowledge. This is not beneficial in any scenario.
> Tribal knowledge is usually knowledge that is verbally shared and is never written down.

I jokingly call this the “lottery bus,” my version of the industry’s “bus factor”: what happens if the only person who knows something suddenly becomes unavailable?

`"Document the steps you took to resolve a niche issue in case you are hit by the “lottery bus” and cannot tell your colleague that passwords should be reset using COMP-DC037 because replication is faster there."`

If you become the lone technician at an MSP supporting multiple clients, having this information documented can make the difference between meeting an SLA and spending hours rediscovering an existing solution.

## Final Thoughts
There are many problems that you'll face when going through your IT career, some of which may not be what I mentioned above. However, I've found that having at least some knowledge from another professional in your field cannot hurt.
